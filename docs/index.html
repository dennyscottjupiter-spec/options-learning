How to read this app

A short, foundational reference for every number this tool shows you. Read it once, then use it as a glossary whenever a term doesn't ring a bell.

What an option actually is

A call is the right (not the obligation) to buy 100 shares at a fixed price (the strike) any time before an expiration date. A put is the right to sell at that fixed price. You pay a premium today for that right — the option's price, quoted per share but always representing a 100-share contract, so a $2.00 premium costs $200 out of pocket.

The four strategies this tool builds

  • Long Call (LEAPS) — buy a call far out (6–24 months) to lock in today's price on a stock you want to own more of later, paying a fraction of the share price instead of the full amount. Max loss is the premium; upside is unlimited.
  • Cash-Secured Put — sell a put, setting aside the full strike × 100 in cash. You collect the premium now. Worst case, you're forced to buy the stock at the strike — a stock you already wanted, at a discount.
  • Covered Call — sell a call against 100 shares you already own. You collect premium; in exchange, you cap your upside above the strike. Turns idle shares into income.
  • Protective Put — buy a put against shares you already own, as insurance. It floors your downside for the cost of the premium, while your upside stays fully open.

Fundamentals panel

Spot price is the stock's most recent close — the number every other calculation in this report is built from. Sector and market cap give quick context on the kind of company and its size. Next earnings matters more than it looks: a surprise earnings move can swing the stock far more than a normal trading day.

Warnings

This tool never hides a bad contract — it flags it and lets you see why. An earnings warning means the contract expires after the next earnings date, so both the price and the probability estimates below are less reliable across that date (implied volatility usually collapses the morning after — an effect called IV crush). A liquidity warning means the bid-ask spread or quoted size is thin, so you'd likely give up more to the spread than a more liquid strike would cost. An affordability warning means the trade needs more capital than your paper account's current buying power.

The contract

  • Strike — the fixed price written into the contract.
  • Breakeven — the strike adjusted for the premium paid or received. This, not the strike, is the price the position actually needs to clear to turn a profit.
  • Premium — what the option costs (or pays you) today, per contract (×100 shares).
  • Max profit / max loss — the best and worst dollar outcomes at expiration.
  • Capital required — cash or margin the position ties up (full strike × 100 for a cash-secured put; the premium for a long call or protective put).

Greeks & implied volatility

The Greeks describe how the option's price reacts to changes in the world — each one isolates one variable.

  • Delta — how much the option's price moves per $1 move in the stock. Roughly doubles as "probability this option finishes in-the-money." A 0.30 delta call behaves like owning 30 shares.
  • Gamma — how fast delta itself changes as the stock moves. High gamma means the position's directional exposure can shift quickly.
  • Theta — how much value the option loses per calendar day, all else equal. This is the "time decay" every options trader has to respect — it works against option buyers and for option sellers.
  • Vega — how much the option's price moves per 1 percentage point change in implied volatility. Long options gain value when volatility rises; short options lose value.
  • Rho — sensitivity to interest rates. Usually the smallest effect of the five, more relevant for long-dated LEAPS than short-dated trades.
  • Implied volatility (IV) — the market's forecast of how much the stock will swing, backed out of the option's price. Higher IV means richer premiums and wider expected moves.

Whenever Alpaca doesn't quote Greeks for a contract (common on illiquid or same-day expirations), this tool computes them locally with its own Black-Scholes engine and labels them "calculated locally" — so you always know whether a number came from the market or from a model.

Probability of profit

This is the section with the highest bar for rigor in the whole report — see the Methodology block beneath it on every report for the exact formula and source file behind each figure below. In plain language:

  • Closed-form (lognormal) — a textbook formula (Black-Scholes' own probability term) assuming the stock's future price follows a lognormal distribution. Fast, exact given that assumption, and the industry-standard first answer.
  • Monte Carlo — instead of trusting the formula, this tool actually simulates 100,000 possible future price paths for the stock and counts how often the position ends profitable. It's the same assumption as the closed-form (geometric Brownian motion), verified by brute force rather than algebra — if the two disagree by more than a hair, the report says so.
  • Probability of touch — closed-form and Monte Carlo both answer "where does the price end up at expiration?" This one asks a different, often more practical question: "does the price touch breakeven at any point before expiration?" It's usually higher than the terminal probability, since a price can touch a level and come back.
  • Average win / average loss — from the same 100,000 simulated paths, the average dollar outcome across the paths that ended profitable, and across the ones that didn't.
  • Risk-adjusted score — combines probability, average win, average loss, and capital at risk into one annualized number, used to rank candidate contracts against each other. It answers "which contract offers the best return per dollar risked per year," not "which contract will win."

One honest caveat that applies to all of the above: under the model's own risk-neutral assumptions, every option's expected profit is approximately zero minus trading costs. These probabilities describe likely outcomes under the market's current pricing — they are not a positive edge. A profitable trade ultimately requires a directional view this model does not have.

Technical snapshot

  • SMA 20 / 50 / 200 — simple moving averages over the last 20, 50, and 200 trading days. Price stacked above rising averages is the classic textbook definition of an uptrend.
  • RSI (14) — Relative Strength Index, a 0–100 momentum gauge. Traditionally, above 70 is considered "overbought" and below 30 "oversold," though strong trends can stay extreme for a long time.
  • Historical volatility (30d / 90d) — how much the stock has actually moved, annualized, over the trailing 30 and 90 days — the realized counterpart to implied volatility. The percentile shown ranks the current reading against the past year, so you can tell if today's volatility is unusually calm or unusually wild for this particular stock.
  • 52-week range — the lowest and highest close in the past year.
  • Directional bias — a simple read from how the moving averages are stacked: bullish, bearish, mixed, or not enough history yet.

Using this tool responsibly

Every trade in this app runs against Alpaca's paper trading account — real market data, zero real money. That's deliberate: the fastest way to build real intuition for options is to watch many contracts play out without the cost of being wrong. Nothing here is investment advice, and no order is ever placed automatically — this is a learning environment for understanding the mechanics before you ever risk a dollar.
